# Evidence notes (for the planner and the coach)

## How to use these notes

- They are short summaries of a few well-known reviews, to keep advice consistent and honest. They are **not** a
  substitute for the owner's own data: research gives averages (mostly short trials, often beginners); the
  logged sets and check-ins show this person. When they disagree, trust the data and say so.
- When a plan or a piece of advice leans on one of these, say so in a few words and state your confidence
  (high, moderate or low). In plan notes keep it to numbers and one short reason; save the longer reasoning for
  the final summary or the chat.
- Do not cite any study that is not listed here unless you are certain it exists and say what it is. Otherwise say
  "general knowledge" and give a confidence. Do not quote figures beyond the ones written here.
- Natural training only. Never suggest PEDs.

## What the evidence says

**1. Weekly volume.** Schoenfeld, Ogborn & Krieger 2017, *J Sports Sci* 35(11):1073-1082
(doi 10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197). More weekly sets per muscle gave more growth, a graded effect with diminishing
returns; higher versus lower volume differed by roughly 4% in percentage gain in their main comparison.
Confidence: moderate to high on the direction, low on any exact optimum. *Use:* if a muscle gets few sets (for example little leg work), adding sets is the first lever. Suggest it; do not restructure routines.

**2. Training frequency.** Schoenfeld, Grgic & Krieger 2019, *J Sports Sci* 37(11):1286-1295. With weekly volume
equated, how many days a muscle is trained made little difference to growth. Confidence: moderate to high.
*Use:* three sessions a week is enough; arrange the volume around the owner's real schedule, and a missed session
is not a disaster.

**3. Proximity to failure.** Refalo et al. 2023, *Sports Med* 53(3):649-665 (doi 10.1007/s40279-022-01784-y).
No clear advantage for training to momentary failure over stopping short of it; the relationship may not be
linear. Confidence: moderate. *Use:* sets do not have to end in failure, but sets stopped very far from failure
probably count for less. The paper does not give a precise cutoff, so do not invent one.

**4. Protein.** Morton et al. 2018, *Br J Sports Med* 52(6):376-384 (doi 10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608). The benefit
of extra protein on lean mass leveled off around 1.6 g per kg of bodyweight per day (higher intakes did little
more). Confidence: moderate to high. *Use:* for 78 kg that is roughly 125 g a day (simple arithmetic).

**5. Cardio alongside lifting.** Schumann et al. 2022, *Sports Med* 52(3):601-612. Adding aerobic training did not
reduce muscle growth or maximal strength on average; explosive strength gains may be blunted, especially when
both are done in the same session. Confidence: moderate. *Use:* weekly cardio sessions are compatible with
lifting. Keep hard cardio away from heavy leg days when possible, and treat running and other cardio as recovery cost:
watch sleep, plan performance and bodyweight trend.

## Not yet verified (general knowledge only)

Safe rate of weight loss, body recomposition, sleep and muscle, natural muscle-gain rates and genetic ceilings.
Speak about these in general terms only, mark them as general knowledge with a confidence, and do not quote
specific studies or numbers for them.

## Goal realism (arithmetic, not a study)

Estimate lean mass as weight x (1 - body fat). Body-fat estimates are rough (a few percentage points either way),
so always give timelines as ranges. With a sample snapshot of 78 kg at about 22%: lean mass is about 61 kg;
at about 11% body fat that would weigh about 68.4 kg, so roughly 10 kg of fat to lose. At 0.5 to 0.75% of
bodyweight per week that is about 4 to 6 months, with strength held or slightly rising. Recompute from the latest
check-ins, and say plainly when a requested timeline is shorter than that arithmetic allows.
